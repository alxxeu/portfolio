ABOUT THOUGHTS

The ideas that don't survive the trip from your head to a note-taking app.

Thoughts is a macOS app for exactly that. It's a free-form canvas where every idea becomes a card — no files, no folders, no decisions to make before you're allowed to start typing.

The problem it solves

Most note apps put five steps between having a thought and writing it down: open the app, create a file, come up with a title, write the text, then decide which folder or tag it belongs to. By the time you've cleared step three, the thought is often already gone. The failure isn't that writing is hard — it's that a thought is easy to lose track of. It gets written down somewhere, drops out of view, and by the time you'd need it again, you can't remember which app it's even in.

This is also personal. For over two years, the workaround was a combination of two unrelated utilities — Plash and manifest.app — just to force notes to live as a permanent desktop wallpaper, always in view, because no note app was built to stay visible rather than to be opened. It turned out this instinct isn't unique: in an open discussion on Plash's own GitHub, other users independently listed note-taking setups — including manifest.app — among the things they pin to their desktop for exactly the same reason. Thoughts is the tool that workaround was standing in for: a Mac's desktop stops being just a bulletin board of icons and becomes an ambient surface for whatever you're actively holding in mind, without a bulletin board icon in sight.

How it works

Drop a thought anywhere on the canvas and it becomes a card — sized and positioned however you want, not queued into a list. Up to nine independent Spaces (⌥1–9) act as entirely separate boards, so a work project and a personal to-do list never bleed into each other. Color tags, rich text, spoiler/lock privacy per card (unlocked with Touch ID), and a passcode per Space cover the organizing and protecting side without ever asking you to leave the canvas.

Two features carry the most weight day to day. Focus Card (⌘F) pulls a single card into the center of the screen — everything else fades away — and lets you ask a question about it directly, powered by Apple Intelligence, ChatGPT, or Claude (your own API key, entirely optional), with the answer dropping straight back into the card. Desktop Overlay takes the idea a step further: it lets Thoughts sit just above your desktop icons instead of in a normal window, so your cards are visible the instant you glance at your desktop — no window to bring forward first. Tidy Cards cleans up a cluttered board with one click, grouping by tag and packing everything into a sensible layout without you dragging a single card by hand.

Why it's built this way

Thoughts isn't trying to compete with Notion, Obsidian, or Apple Notes — it isn't in the same category. Those are archives: built for documents you'll return to over months or years, and they all ask you to make a filing decision before you're allowed to write. Thoughts is built for the opposite case — the fast, temporary, currently-relevant thought — and it's deliberately unopinionated about long-term structure, because that's not the problem it's solving.

Privacy follows the same instinct: nothing about this app should ask for trust it hasn't earned. There's no account, no analytics, no server, and no third-party SDK — verified independently by Apple's own "Data Not Collected" privacy label, not just claimed in a description. Even the AI features are opt-in and run either fully on-device (Apple Intelligence) or directly from your Mac to the provider you choose, using a key only you hold.

None of this is a new habit — sticky notes on a monitor, a corkboard in an office, notes stuck to a fridge have solved the same problem for decades: keep the thought where you can see it, don't file it away. Thoughts is a considered, private, and genuinely spatial version of that same, much older idea — built because an existing gap in how Macs handle fleeting thoughts was solved with duct tape for two years, and it seemed worth actually solving properly instead.

ABOUT REUP

A marketplace built to make secondhand fashion feel as easy to trust as buying new.

REUP is an AI-powered marketplace for sustainable, secondhand and upcycled fashion, built end-to-end as a UX/UI case study in 2023 and accepted the same year into the Skolkovo innovation program.

The problem it solves

Secondhand and upcycled fashion is scattered across marketplaces, flea markets and one-off resale accounts, which makes shopping sustainably feel like more effort than fast fashion. REUP's premise was to make responsible fashion as easy to browse and trust as a regular online store — one catalog for vintage, curated secondhand, upcycled pieces and small local labels — with AI doing the part that's hardest to replicate offline: judging fit and personal style before you buy.

How it works

The catalog is organized the way people actually shop secondhand: by category (outerwear, shoes, bags, dresses...) and by curation type — vintage, selective secondhand, upcycled pieces, and listings from other users. AI personalization surfaces a "picked for you" row on the homepage, alongside editorial "REUP's choice" features and themed collections, so the store feels edited rather than just searchable. Virtual fitting lets shoppers preview how a specific secondhand piece — with its one-off fit and condition — will actually look on them, which is the single biggest trust gap in buying used clothing online.

My role & what I did

As the UX/UI designer, I owned the full design cycle: competitor analysis and user research, user scenarios and information architecture, interactive prototypes and A/B testing, and a UI system and component library built from scratch. I also designed and built an internal CRM so the team could manage listings, sellers and orders, and worked directly with developers, marketers and business analysts on key product decisions.

Where it stands

REUP passed every stage of the Skolkovo program in 2023, which validated both the concept and the execution — but the project didn't secure the funding it needed to launch publicly, and development was paused.
